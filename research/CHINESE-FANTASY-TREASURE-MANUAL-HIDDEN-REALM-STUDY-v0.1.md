# Chinese Fantasy Treasure, Manual & Hidden-Realm Study v0.1

**Date:** 2026-10-03  
**Status:** Research / inspiration analysis — not canonical lore  
**Scope:** Transferable structures from Chinese fantasy/xianxia for original Adventurer Life RPG systems.

## Research boundary

This document studies why treasures, manuals, inheritances, rare materials and secret realms create long-term curiosity.

It does **not** authorize copying:
- named artifacts;
- cultivation realms;
- named techniques;
- sects;
- characters;
- scenes;
- dialogue;
- maps;
- proprietary cosmology.

All final Oravel content must be original.

# 2. Strong recurring pattern — treasure is a story object

Memorable treasure usually has several of these properties:

1. **Provenance** — someone made/owned/lost it.
2. **Uncertainty** — the current owner does not immediately know everything.
3. **Condition** — it needs a key, skill, material, state or location.
4. **Specificity** — it solves a particular problem unusually well.
5. **Growth** — it can be repaired, completed, refined or understood.
6. **Connection** — it points toward another person/place/manual/realm.
7. **Opportunity cost** — using, selling or consuming it closes another option.
8. **Social value** — other people recognize or desire it.
9. **Risk** — ownership may attract attention or require responsibility.
10. **Memory** — the player remembers how it was obtained.

This is more powerful than rarity color alone.

# 3. 凡人修仙传 — ordinary person, extraordinary resource network

The useful design lesson from *A Record of a Mortal's Journey to Immortality* is not to copy cultivation.

The structural appeal is that progression is tightly connected to **resources, information, preparation and trade**.

Examples observed in licensed text:

- spirit stones function as exchange currency;
- spirit stones also provide energy and support formations;
- rare ancient treasures may be effectively unavailable in normal markets;
- valuable medicines/materials can be traded through internal markets;
- very rare exchanges can demand a specific material plus currency;
- large auctions expose long catalogues of desirable objects.

## Transferable lesson

A resource becomes more interesting when it has both:

> **exchange value + functional value**

Oravel should keep normal coin readable, while some magical resources can create real "sell or use?" decisions.

---

# 4. I Shall Seal the Heavens — legacy as an adventure structure

Observed structures include:

- legacy zones;
- restrictive trials;
- artifacts tied to legacy;
- clues gathered from old records;
- preparation before attempting acquisition;
- treasure and inheritance existing together.

## Transferable lesson

A treasure can be the **endpoint of a mini-story**, not random loot.

Original Oravel structure:

> old record -> location clue -> access condition -> trials -> artifact/manual -> new unanswered clue

This fits Underfold side-nodes and abandoned schools extremely well.

---

# 5. Renegade Immortal — ownership, refinement and incomplete knowledge

Observed structures include:

- treasures of differing authenticity/quality;
- pseudo or imperfect versions;
- refining knowledge;
- restrictions placed on items;
- inheritances with uncertain motives/conditions.

## Transferable lesson

Item identity can have depth:

- replica;
- damaged original;
- incomplete original;
- reconstructed version;
- authentic relic;
- modified relic.

The best version need not simply have a larger damage number.

---

# 6. Desolate Era — treasury as challenge, not shop

Observed structures include large estates/treasuries where:

- many treasures exist;
- access requires trials;
- information can be purchased before entry;
- different challenges test different capabilities;
- rare objects may be useful to particular bonded weapons or goals.

## Transferable lesson

A treasury can ask:

> "Which reward matters to your build/life?"

rather than:

> "Which chest has the highest item level?"

Secret areas should contain choices, incomplete information and specialist value.

---

# 7. A Will Eternal — techniques and pills as culture

The licensed glossary demonstrates a broad ecology of:

- secret legacy techniques;
- sect-specific arts;
- pills with specialized functions;
- techniques with multiple internal stages.

## Transferable lesson

Techniques should have:

- origin;
- school/tradition;
- theory;
- training stages;
- specialist purpose.

Do not make every skill a generic icon purchased from one universal trainer.

# 8. Treasure Desire Model

Before adding a rare treasure, score its design qualitatively across seven questions.

## T1 — Mystery
Does the player have something meaningful to discover about it?

## T2 — Utility
Does it enable a distinctive action, route or strategy?

## T3 — Provenance
Does it have a believable history?

## T4 — Connection
Does it connect to another clue, person, region or system?

## T5 — Choice
Does keeping/using/selling/altering it involve a real decision?

## T6 — Growth
Can understanding, restoring or combining it change its value?

## T7 — Memory
Is its acquisition likely to become a story the player remembers?

A treasure with high raw power but zero answers to these questions is probably just gear.

# 9. Proposed original Oravel treasure taxonomy

This taxonomy is intentionally independent of Chinese cultivation tiers.

## Tier A — Curiosity
Odd object whose significance is unknown.

Examples:
- sealed compass;
- unidentified plate;
- damaged lens;
- map fragment.

## Tier B — Specialist Treasure
Valuable to a profession or build.

Examples:
- rare smithing hammer;
- Pattern calibration crystal;
- monster-research instrument.

## Tier C — Named Relic
Documented historical object with provenance.

## Tier D — Legacy Relic
Connected to a lost person, school, expedition or civilization.

## Tier E — World Relic
Connected to the buried world network or major world mystery.

These are narrative categories, not guaranteed combat-power tiers.

# 10. Manuals / codices / technique records

Working original categories:

## Field Notes
Practical techniques and observations.

## Training Manual
Teaches a repeatable technique.

## School Codex
Contains a coherent discipline or philosophy.

## Fragmented Codex
Missing sections; finding more pieces matters.

## Legacy Record
Technique plus history/trial/inheritance.

## Pattern Treatise
Advanced magical theory.

## Forbidden / Restricted Record
Dangerous, illegal or institutionally controlled knowledge.

Restrictions should have in-world reasons.

---

# 11. Manual learning model

Reading should not instantly upload mastery.

Possible process:

1. acquire;
2. identify language;
3. understand prerequisites;
4. study;
5. practice;
6. fail safely;
7. seek mentor or missing context;
8. demonstrate technique;
9. master variants.

This creates progression without another vertical level ladder.

---

# 12. Technique fragments

A technique can be split across:

- theory;
- stance;
- breathing/rhythm;
- execution;
- recovery;
- advanced application.

A fragment may still be useful.

This prevents "incomplete = worthless."

Example original design:

**River-Splitting Spear Record**
- Fragment I: footwork.
- Fragment II: force transfer.
- Fragment III: recovery.
- Complete understanding unlocks an advanced interaction.

Name is a placeholder and must pass later originality/naming review.

---

# 13. Treasure identification

Not every property should be visible instantly.

Information layers:

- visual inspection;
- profession appraisal;
- Guild archive;
- Pattern analysis;
- historical research;
- field activation;
- specialist NPC/player;
- linked location.

Avoid fake randomness.

Unknown properties should be discoverable through evidence.

---

# 14. Artifact growth without gear treadmill

Possible growth paths:

- restore missing component;
- repair;
- discover true function;
- attune;
- translate inscription;
- learn compatible technique;
- find original sheath/focus/key;
- return to original site;
- combine with related fragment.

This lets a beloved item survive for years.

---

# 15. Rare materials

Rare materials should have ecological/geographic logic.

Categories:

- biological;
- mineral;
- meteorological;
- Pattern-condensed;
- ancient manufactured;
- monster-derived;
- environmental anomaly;
- recovered old-world material.

A material entry needs:
- origin;
- harvesting method;
- renewability;
- danger;
- uses;
- substitutes;
- trade constraints.

---

# 16. Medicines / alchemy

Avoid "red potion, blue potion, bigger red potion" as the entire system.

Possible families:

- emergency stabilization;
- antidote;
- fatigue management;
- recovery support;
- temperature resistance;
- breathing/diving;
- Pattern interference protection;
- sensory aid;
- expedition preservation.

Powerful medicines can require:
- rare ingredients;
- specialist craft;
- shelf-life;
- preparation;
- medical risk.

Do not build addictive real-time consumption loops.

# 17. Hidden Realm Model

A hidden realm is a **bounded adventure space with a discovery story**.

Types:

## Sealed Node
Old buried-network branch.

## Lost Workshop
Craft/research site.

## Legacy Ground
Training or inheritance site.

## Environmental Pocket
Unusual ecology.

## Temporal / Spatial Anomaly
Use sparingly and establish rules.

## Lost Settlement
History/social discovery.

---

# 18. Hidden-realm access model

Access should combine clues rather than raw luck.

Potential requirements:

- map fragment;
- world condition;
- item;
- language;
- profession knowledge;
- reputation;
- route;
- puzzle;
- party composition;
- previous discovery.

Critical shared-world rule:

**Finding the entrance first must not permanently deny everyone else meaningful access.**

Possible solution:
- discovery prestige is global;
- story instance is personal/party;
- resource layer replenishes;
- server events reopen access.

---

# 19. Inheritance design

Inheritance should not mean "free ultimate power."

Possible rewards:

- knowledge;
- technique;
- responsibility;
- workshop;
- relationship;
- unfinished mission;
- artifact custody;
- map;
- access right.

A great inheritance can create new work instead of ending progression.

# 20. Auctions — why they are fun in fiction

Auction scenes combine:

- anticipation;
- information;
- wealth comparison;
- unexpected objects;
- rivals;
- hidden value;
- budgeting;
- social status;
- rumors.

For Oravel:

### Public catalogue
Players can inspect most lots beforehand.

### Unknown lots
A small number can remain partially identified.

### Proxy bidding
No forced fixed-hour attendance.

### Requested exchange
Seller can request:
- coin;
- material category;
- artifact category;
- mixed offer.

### Appraisal
Buyers may pay experts to evaluate an object.

### Provenance
Auction record becomes part of the item's history.

---

# 21. Avoid pay-to-win auction psychology

Never use:
- real-money bidding for combat relics;
- artificial scarcity designed to provoke spending;
- countdown pressure for essential power;
- hidden odds.

Auction excitement should come from the fictional economy and discovery.

# 22. Treasure examples for Oravel

Original prototypes only.

## Compass of the Dry River
Appears broken in ordinary terrain.
Near buried water-control structures, its needle points toward pressure flow rather than north.

Value:
- exploration;
- world mystery;
- mapping.

Not a weapon.

## Bell of Returning Echoes
Records a short acoustic "memory" of a location under specific Pattern conditions.

Uses:
- investigation;
- archaeology;
- puzzle clues.

## Ashglass Needle
Fine tool used by old Pattern engineers.

Uses:
- diagnose damaged magical infrastructure;
- specialist crafting.

## The Unfinished Atlas
Pages reveal routes only after the player personally verifies landmarks.

It becomes a long-term collection object.

## Grey Orchard Seed
Ancient seed that cannot germinate in ordinary soil.

Finding the correct environmental pocket opens a noncombat quest chain.

# 23. Treasure collection should tell a biography

Player collection UI should support:

- where found;
- date/era;
- party members present;
- appraisal history;
- previous owners;
- restoration work;
- discoveries unlocked;
- display location.

The collection becomes the player's adventure diary.

---

# 24. Treasure fairness in multiplayer

Rare does not have to mean only one player on the server can ever own it.

Use several rarity dimensions:

- lore rarity;
- acquisition difficulty;
- cosmetic uniqueness;
- provenance uniqueness;
- server-first prestige;
- mechanical rarity.

Example:

The **first discovered original** can be historically unique, while later players can:
- study it;
- earn replicas;
- find related originals;
- access equivalent gameplay.

Do not make one player permanently own a required Main Story key.

---

# 25. Long-term loop

A veteran player should be able to pursue:

> rumor -> research -> preparation -> expedition -> treasure -> appraisal -> technique/collection/trade decision -> new clue

This loop can survive beyond Rank S without inventing Rank SS.

---

# 26. Strong design conclusion

The best Chinese-fantasy lesson for Adventurer Life RPG is:

> **Power progression is most compelling when the world contains things worth knowing, finding, trading, restoring and mastering—not merely stronger enemies.**

Treasure should create stories.

Manuals should create learning journeys.

Hidden realms should create curiosity.

Auctions should reveal what the world values.

Rare materials should connect ecology, crafting and geography.

And none of these systems require players to become enemies.
