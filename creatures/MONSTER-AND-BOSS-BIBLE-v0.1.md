# Monster & Boss Bible v0.1

**Date:** 2026-10-02  
**Status:** Creature design framework; individual bestiary not yet complete

## Core principle

> **A monster is part of a world before it is an enemy stat block.**

Every important creature should answer:

1. What does it eat?
2. Where does it live?
3. Why is it here?
4. What threatens it?
5. What does it compete with?
6. What changes if players remove too many?
7. What useful/dangerous materials come from it?
8. Can the conflict be solved without killing it?

---

# 1. Creature categories

## Wildlife
Ordinary fantasy ecology.

Examples:
- grazers;
- predators;
- scavengers;
- insects;
- river creatures.

## Drift-Touched
Creatures changed by local magical conditions.

Not automatically evil.

## Dungeon-Adapted
Species that evolved or settled inside buried structures.

## Construct / Remnant
Artificial organisms or machines associated with old infrastructure.

## Intelligent Peoples / Hostile Groups
Never label an entire sapient species "monster" merely because combat exists.

## Ancient Entity
Rare beings tied to world history or buried-network systems.

---

# 2. Ecology-driven quest chains

Example:

Slimes consume organic runoff.

If slime numbers fall sharply:

1. runoff accumulates;
2. insects increase;
3. predators follow the insects;
4. farms experience new threats;
5. the Guild posts investigation and containment work.

This creates story through ecology rather than endless random spawning.

---

# 3. Threat design

A creature's danger is not only HP/damage.

Threat can include:

- poison;
- terrain control;
- night activity;
- pack behavior;
- camouflage;
- disease;
- equipment damage;
- sound attraction;
- water contamination;
- magical interference;
- intelligence.

This supports noncombat preparation and specialist roles.

# 4. Boss classes

## 4.1 Contract Boss
Local high-risk target.

Examples:
- territorial predator;
- nest leader;
- dangerous criminal creature.

Scale: F–C most commonly.

## 4.2 Dungeon Guardian
Creature/construct tied to a route, chamber or system.

Victory may involve:

- defeat;
- bypass;
- deactivate;
- repair;
- lure away;
- satisfy a condition.

## 4.3 Named Monster
Unique or near-unique legendary encounter.

The monster has:

- identity;
- history;
- evidence trail;
- recognizable behavior;
- discovery conditions.

Named Monsters should be stories, not low-drop-rate spawn tables.

## 4.4 World Threat
Large event requiring multiple parties or community contribution.

Examples:
- migration disaster;
- giant creature;
- regional infestation;
- awakened buried mechanism.

## 4.5 Ancient Entity
Rare Main Story-linked beings.

They may be:

- sentient;
- misunderstood;
- remnants;
- guardians;
- transformed survivors;
- parts of the old network.

Do not assume the correct solution is always to kill them.

---

# 5. Named Monster design pattern

A Named Monster encounter should have:

### Rumor
People tell imperfect stories.

### Evidence
Tracks, damaged objects, remains, weather or environmental effects.

### Pattern
The creature behaves coherently.

### Condition
Players can infer when/where it appears.

### Confrontation
Combat or another resolution.

### Legacy
The encounter changes:

- a route;
- local story;
- title;
- research;
- crafting;
- faction opinion;
- future quests.

# 6. Example original Named Monster concepts

These are placeholders for development, not final locked canon.

## The Lantern Eater
Rumor: travelers lose lights before disappearances.

Truth direction:
- attracted to unstable/dying light rather than bright light;
- follows particular heat/drift signatures.

Possible resolutions:
- kill;
- trap;
- relocate;
- understand why its normal habitat collapsed.

## The Reed Crown
A massive wetland predator whose migration follows river pressure changes.

Gameplay:
- tracking;
- village evacuation;
- baiting;
- environmental manipulation.

## The Stone Sleeper
Ancient construct mistaken for a rock formation.

Possible trigger:
- pressure gate activation.

May be repairable rather than hostile by nature.

# 7. Boss encounter principles

Good bosses test more than damage:

- preparation;
- observation;
- movement;
- positioning;
- resource use;
- party communication;
- environmental knowledge.

Avoid mandatory one-shot knowledge checks with no warning.

A failed attempt should often teach something usable.

---

# 8. Dungeon ecology progression

Existing ten-strata structure remains a high-level scaffold.

Potential ecological escalation:

- Floors 1–10: surface-adjacent life, slimes, pests, opportunistic predators.
- 11–20: mine-adapted species, scavengers, territorial groups.
- 21–30: underground wild ecosystem.
- 31–40: flooded/sunken environments.
- 41–50: corrupted/artificial habitats.
- 51–60: unstable drift ecology.
- 61–70: apex scavengers and ancient remains.
- 71–80: life around lost civilization zones.
- 81–90: ecology begins violating surface assumptions.
- 91–99: poorly documented systems and entities.
- 100: narrative junction; not automatically a final combat arena.

Exact bestiary remains future work.

# 9. Monster materials

Creature materials may support:

- cooking;
- medicine;
- alchemy;
- armor;
- weapons;
- research;
- tools;
- decoration;
- trade.

Important rule:

> A valuable monster should not require destructive overfarming if the world fiction says the species is rare.

Possible alternatives:

- shed materials;
- nonlethal harvest;
- timed collection;
- breeding/farming only where appropriate;
- Guild conservation rules.

---

# 10. Social safety

Avoid creating systems that reward players for ruining another player's access to a rare creature.

For shared-world Named Monsters:

- use personal/party credit;
- instancing or contribution credit;
- non-exclusive discovery records;
- repeatable story access where necessary;
- server-first discovery can award prestige without permanently denying content.

Detailed economy/social rules belong to a later system document.
